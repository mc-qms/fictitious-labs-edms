# SOP-QA-025 — Viral Clearance

**Fictitious Labs Inc.** · Version 1.0 · Effective 2026-03-02 · Owner: Quality Control

## 1. Purpose

Establish how the capacity of the manufacturing process to remove and inactivate viruses is evaluated, validated, and maintained, as one part of the overall viral safety strategy.

## 2. Scope

Applies to viral clearance studies and their maintenance for products manufactured at Fictitious Labs Inc., a US-based biologics organization manufacturing monoclonal antibody drug substance in the United States, in the following functions: Quality Control laboratory, Quality Assurance, Manufacturing.

Does not cover adventitious-agent testing of banks and harvests itself, which follows SOP-QC-004, Cell Bank Management, or raw material viral safety qualification, which follows SOP-QA-015, Supplier Quality.

## 3. Responsibilities

- **Study Director**
  - A Quality Control scientist who owns each viral clearance study: design the study, own the protocol and the report, document the virus-selection and scale-down rationale, and assess study deviations and results.

- **Laboratory Scientist**
  - Execute the studies and assays as the protocol directs, record the data contemporaneously, and report deviations or unexpected results to the Study Director.

- **Quality Assurance**
  - Approve each protocol before execution and each report before its conclusions are used. Approve the use of prior knowledge, alternative viruses, and exceptions under this procedure, and determine the change-control route for re-evaluation.

## 4. Procedure

Viral safety relies on three complementary elements: controlled starting materials, testing at defined stages, and validated viral clearance. No element substitutes for another. This procedure covers viral clearance studies and their maintenance. Starting-material controls are managed through SOP-QA-015, Supplier Quality, and testing at defined stages through SOP-QC-004, Cell Bank Management.

### 4.1 Plan the study

1. Assess potential viral risks.
2. Define the study question using available information on the cell line, cell-bank and unprocessed-bulk test results, culture and process conditions, and the product. Base the level of effort and documentation on the risk.
3. Use applicable test results to inform the study plan. Obtain those results through SOP-QC-004, Cell Bank Management; this procedure does not establish how to test banks or harvests.
4. Document the rationale for the study design, including the viruses selected, process steps evaluated, scale-down approach, assays, and how the results will be used to assess clearance.
5. Identify manufacturing steps likely to remove or inactivate viruses.
6. Evaluate those steps individually where appropriate, and assess their contribution to overall clearance.
7. Consider the amount of virus that may enter the process, including available information on unprocessed bulk, and the amount the process can clear. For inactivation steps, evaluate how inactivation changes with exposure time.
8. The Study Director prepares the study protocol.
9. Quality Assurance approves the protocol before execution, and the approval is recorded in FL-EDMS. No study is executed before that approval is complete.

### 4.2 Select viruses and assays

1. Select and justify viruses that represent likely contaminants and a range of physical and chemical properties. Use relevant viruses when available and suitable. Use specific model viruses when relevant viruses are unavailable or unsuitable, and non-specific model viruses to characterize process robustness. The Study Director decides whether a virus is suitable and which model-virus path applies, with Quality Assurance approval; the selected path and rationale are recorded in the protocol.
2. Generally evaluate at least three viruses with differing characteristics. Include non-enveloped viruses in the study design and, where feasible, select viruses resistant to physical or chemical treatments.
3. When a virus or virus-like particle is identified in cells or unprocessed bulk, apply the pathway that matches the virus finding. If the virus is known and there is no evidence of human infectivity, use the identified virus for clearance studies. If it cannot be used, use relevant or specific model viruses; obtain time-dependent inactivation data for the identified virus or the selected model viruses at critical inactivation steps, and test purified bulk with suitable methods specific and sensitive for the virus. If a virus infectious to humans is identified, treat its use as an exceptional circumstance, not a routine pathway. Use the identified virus for clearance studies and specific methods with high specificity and sensitivity for its detection. If the identified virus cannot be used, use relevant and/or specific model viruses, and demonstrate removal and inactivation during downstream processing, including time-dependent inactivation data for critical inactivation steps. Test purified bulk with suitable specific and sensitive methods. If a detected virus cannot be classified by available methods, the product is usually considered unacceptable; in the rare case of well-justified reasons for production, discuss the case with the appropriate regulatory authorities before proceeding further. When the identified virus cannot be used, the Study Director documents the reason for selecting an alternative, with Quality Assurance approval, in the protocol or study report.
4. When a production virus is used, evaluate its clearance or use a specific model virus if the production virus cannot be used. Determine the production-virus level in unprocessed bulk using at least three cell-culture lots or batches. Test purified bulk for residual production virus unless robust excess clearance justifies not testing each purified bulk.
5. Quality Assurance decides whether robust excess clearance justifies this exception, on the Study Director's justification recorded in the study report. When the exception is used, confirm absence in at least three purified-bulk batches.
6. Use reliable, sensitive, reproducible quantitative infectivity assays for each virus and process stage assessed. Use a non-infectivity assay only when justified. Include appropriate virus controls and assess sample toxicity or assay interference.

### 4.3 Establish the scale-down model

1. Demonstrate that the scale-down model represents the manufacturing process, including comparable yield and purity.
2. Document any differences and assess their effect on the study conclusions. The acceptance criteria for model representativeness are defined in the protocol.
3. The Study Director assesses representativeness against them.
4. Quality Assurance approves the assessment.
5. For chromatography, establish comparability for bed height, linear flow rate, flow rate-to-bed-volume ratio (contact time), buffer and resin types, pH, temperature, conductivity, product concentration, and chromatogram.
6. Match spiking volume and process exposure times to manufacturing conditions. Avoid virus aggregation during preparation and use.
7. Consider worst-case process conditions and robustness when defining the model and study conditions.

### 4.4 Conduct the study

1. Conduct documented, controlled studies in a separate virology laboratory with virology experts and production personnel.
2. Spike a significant amount of virus into unprocessed bulk or an appropriate process intermediate.
3. Measure virus levels before and after each assessed step.
4. For each step, describe whether the observed loss of infectivity results from removal, inactivation, or both.
5. Assess virus distribution across separation fractions where appropriate and feasible.
6. For inactivation steps, collect samples at different exposure times to construct an inactivation curve. Include at least one sample time greater than zero and shorter than the minimum exposure time, as well as a sample at the minimum exposure time.
7. Use sufficient assay replicates for statistical validity and include parallel controls to assess losses during sample handling.
8. Demonstrate reproducibility in at least two independent experiments, unless the reduction is justified by prior knowledge.
9. Quality Assurance decides whether prior knowledge justifies this exception, and the justification is recorded in the study report.
10. The Study Director assesses each study deviation and documents it in the study record.
11. A deviation with a possible product or process impact is routed under SOP-QA-010, Handling of Deviations.

### 4.5 Calculate and assess clearance

1. Calculate each step's reduction factor from the ratio of pre-step and post-step virus loads, using titres and volumes: the reduction factor is the total virus load (titre × volume) before the step divided by the total virus load after it, expressed in log10.
2. Calculate the overall reduction factor as the sum of the individual step reduction factors expressed as log values.
3. Use an individual reduction factor in the overall reduction only when the factor itself is greater than 1; calculate the overall reduction factor as the sum of the logarithms of the individual step reduction factors. Do not sum reductions that are not significant. Disregard reductions below 1 log10 unless justified. Reductions of 1 to 3 log10 may contribute to overall reduction. An effective step generally provides reproducible reduction in the order of 4 log10 or more in at least two independent studies.
4. For relevant and specific model viruses, calculate 95% confidence limits for observed reduction factors wherever possible. Within-assay 95% confidence limits normally should be on the order of ±0.5 log10 of the mean. For between-assay variation, include a reference preparation; its potency estimate should be within approximately 0.5 log10 of the laboratory mean for the assay to be acceptable.
5. Document justification for lower-precision assays. Confidence limits, reference-preparation potency, and justifications are recorded in the study report.
6. Quality Assurance, with the Study Director, decides how to handle results that do not meet this guidance.
7. If only part of a sample is tested and the result is negative, calculate the virus amount that would be present in the total sample to achieve a positive result and account for it in the reduction factor.
8. Assess whether the results support the proposed clearance strategy. Prefer two distinct, complementary clearance steps where feasible and where they do not adversely affect the product; include a step effective against non-enveloped virus. Justify the acceptability of reduction factors.
9. Where resin reuse is proposed, support reuse with clearance studies or justified equivalent prior knowledge. Ensure retained virus is removed or inactivated before reuse.

### 4.6 Apply prior knowledge and maintain the assessment

1. Apply prior knowledge only when the study step and process are representative. Otherwise, perform product-specific studies.
2. For platform use, demonstrate robust clearance across products made under established, well-characterized conditions. Show that process intermediates are comparable to those used in the clearance studies, unless prior knowledge supports robustness to differences in composition.
3. Document the comparison and rationale.
4. Assess the direct and indirect effects of significant manufacturing changes on viral clearance.
5. Re-evaluate clearance when needed, using SOP-QA-004, Change Control.
6. The Study Director initiates and completes the review.
7. Quality Assurance determines the applicable change-control route.
8. Review the viral clearance assessment when planned or unplanned events could affect its conclusions, including manufacturing changes or study findings.
9. The Study Director, with Quality Assurance, determines whether an event could affect the conclusions.
10. The assessment is reviewed on trigger and on a schedule of at least every 3 years, and the decisions are recorded in FL-EDMS.
11. Fictitious Labs does not operate continuous manufacturing, and the continuous-manufacturing provisions for unit-operation risk assessment, disturbance monitoring, and dynamic inactivation control are not applicable. If a continuous process is proposed, those provisions are assessed and this procedure is revised through SOP-QA-004 before the process is used.

## 5. Records

Viral clearance records, including risk assessments, protocols and approvals, virus-selection and scale-down rationales, study data and reports, reduction-factor calculations, exception justifications, and re-evaluation reviews, retained in FL-EDMS under the retention requirements of SOP-QA-001, Good Documentation Practices.
