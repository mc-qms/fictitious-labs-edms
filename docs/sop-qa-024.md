# SOP-QA-024 — Aseptic Processing and Media Fills

**Fictitious Labs Inc.** · Version 1.0 · Effective 2026-03-02 · Owner: Manufacturing

## 1. Purpose

Establish how aseptic operations are designed, performed, and monitored, including aseptic process simulations (media fills), so that sterile product is protected from contamination at every step.

## 2. Scope

Applies to aseptic processing, sterile filtration, and media fills at Fictitious Labs Inc., a US-based biologics organization manufacturing monoclonal antibody drug substance in the United States, in the following functions: Manufacturing, Microbiology laboratory, Quality Assurance.

Does not cover terminal sterilization processes, which are validated under SOP-QA-016, Process Validation, or sterility testing itself.

## 3. Responsibilities

- **Aseptic Operator**
  - Follow approved aseptic practices.
  - Complete assigned training and gowning qualification.
  - Perform assigned monitoring.
  - Record activities and interventions as they occur.

- **Manufacturing Supervisor**
  - Ensure that qualified personnel and approved processes are used for aseptic operations.
  - Ensure that operator numbers do not exceed the established maximum.
  - Ensure that deviations and monitoring results are escalated for assessment.

- **Microbiologist**
  - Support contamination-control design, environmental monitoring, aseptic process simulation planning and evaluation, and microbiological investigations.

- **Quality Assurance**
  - Oversee the contamination control strategy and monitoring program.
  - Review relevant excursions and investigations.
  - Assess whether changes may affect contamination control.

## 4. Procedure

### 4.1 Contamination control and process design

1. Sterility is built into the process; do not rely on testing to create or assure sterility. The contamination control strategy (CCS) governs facility and process design, personnel behavior, controls, and monitoring as one system. Monitoring or testing alone does not assure sterility.
2. Use quality risk management to identify, evaluate, and control contamination risks. Consider microbial, particulate, and endotoxin/pyrogen sources, including personnel, materials, equipment, utilities, premises, and process activities.
3. Design and qualify facilities, equipment, and processes before relying on procedures and monitoring to demonstrate continued control.
4. Document the CCS, its controls, and how their combined effectiveness is assessed.
5. The CCS is a controlled document maintained in FL-EDMS by Manufacturing with QC Microbiology input.
6. The CCS is approved by Quality Assurance.
7. Review the CCS at least annually and when changes may affect contamination control.

### 4.2 Facility, equipment, and utilities

1. Use qualified cleanrooms with filtered air and controlled personnel and material transfers.
2. Minimize direct operator intervention in Grade A.
3. Use a restricted access barrier system (RABS) or isolator where appropriate. Fictitious Labs' aseptic operations use Grade A critical zones with a Grade B background and no isolator.
4. Document the rationale for the selected approach in the CCS.
5. For aseptic processing, Grade A is the critical zone. Use Grade B as the background where no isolator is used.
6. Set the required grades and supporting controls for other areas through the CCS.
7. Maintain positive pressure or airflow toward lower-grade backgrounds. The guidance value for the pressure difference between adjacent grades is at least 10 Pascals.
8. Continuously monitor critical pressure differences.
9. Continuously record critical pressure differences.
10. Alarm instantly on supply failure or pressure below critical set limits. Assess and justify any alarm delay; do not override a warning without assessment. Critical set limits are those established in each area's qualification.
11. On a pressure or supply alarm the operator and the Manufacturing Supervisor respond.
12. On a pressure or supply alarm Quality Assurance assesses product impact with SOP-QC-001, Environmental Monitoring.
13. Any alarm-delay assessment is recorded in FL-EDMS.
14. Assess transfers for contamination risk.
15. Transfer items into Grade A/B unidirectionally, preferably through double-ended equipment after sterilization.
16. If that approach is not practicable, validate a process that achieves the same objective.
17. Transfer only approved, validated items into Grade A/B.
18. Pre-approve exceptions, and assess their risks and mitigations before transfer. Use a QA-approved disinfection and monitoring programme for exceptions.
19. Record the risks and mitigations for exceptions before transfer.
20. Quality Assurance approves each exception and its risk assessment before transfer.
21. The exception is recorded in FL-EDMS.
22. Do not open airlock entry and exit doors at the same time.
23. Maintain equipment, air handling, and water systems under qualification, monitoring, and planned maintenance.
24. Sterilize direct and indirect product-contact parts used in aseptic processes.
25. Assess the potential sterility impact of unplanned maintenance on equipment critical to product sterility.
26. Record the potential sterility impact of unplanned maintenance on equipment critical to product sterility.
27. Approve equipment return to use after maintenance.

### 4.3 Personnel and aseptic practices

1. Allow only the minimum number of personnel needed in cleanrooms.
2. Establish the maximum operator number for each area.
3. Document the maximum operator number for each area.
4. Before permitting entry or planning an APS, use the applicable documented maximum. The maximum operator number for each area is set in the CCS. Consider these maxima during qualification and APS.
5. Train personnel who enter cleanrooms according to their function and the criticality of the area. Training must cover microbiology, hygiene, cleanroom practices, contamination control, aseptic technique, and product protection.
6. Gown-qualify personnel who enter cleanrooms according to their function and the criticality of the area.
7. Assess personnel who enter cleanrooms according to their function and the criticality of the area.
8. Assess gowning compliance and reassess it at least annually using visual and microbial assessment. A gowning assessment passes when the visual assessment is satisfactory and microbial results are within the approved levels for the grade under SOP-QC-001.
9. Restrict unsupervised access to Grade A/B areas where aseptic operations occur to qualified personnel who have passed gowning assessment and participated in a successful APS. Do not admit unqualified personnel to Grade B or Grade A in operation except under written procedures for exceptional cases.
10. Exceptional access is authorized and supervised by the Manufacturing Supervisor with Quality Assurance concurrence.
11. The authorized person supervises the access and assesses its cleanliness impact.
12. The access and assessment are recorded in FL-EDMS.
13. Use written gowning and handwashing procedures.
14. Check garments for cleanliness and integrity before and after gowning, and check integrity again on exit.
15. For Grade A/B entry, use clean, sterilized, correctly sized protective garments, including eye coverings and masks.
16. Disinfect gloves regularly.
17. Immediately change damaged garments or gloves that present a contamination risk.
18. Move slowly and deliberately. Maintain aseptic technique, avoid disturbing airflow, restrict movement beside the critical zone, and do not obstruct first-air protection.

### 4.4 Cleaning and disinfection

1. Clean before disinfection and remove residues that could reduce disinfectant effectiveness.
2. Use more than one type of disinfectant and periodically use a sporicide.
3. Validate disinfection, including surface suitability and in-use expiry.
4. Monitor disinfection effectiveness and changes in microbial flora.
5. Use sterile disinfectants and detergents in Grade A/B.
6. Prepare and store on-site solutions to protect them from contamination.
7. Monitor on-site solutions.
8. Use on-site solutions only within a defined period.
9. The cleaning and disinfection program — approved agents, rotation, use conditions, and in-use periods — is maintained by QC Microbiology.
10. The cleaning and disinfection program — approved agents, rotation, use conditions, and in-use periods — is approved by Quality Assurance.

### 4.5 Sterile filtration

1. Apply sterile filtration as part of the contamination-control process; do not treat it as a substitute for controlling the preceding and following aseptic steps.
2. Sterile filtration uses sterilizing-grade filters selected for the product and process.
3. Filters are integrity-tested after use, and before use after sterilization where the CCS requires it.
4. A filtration failure is handled as a deviation under SOP-QA-010, Handling of Deviations, with product impact assessed by Quality Assurance.
5. For product-contact or primary-container-contact gases used in aseptic processes, filter the gas at the point of use through a sterilizing-grade filter with a maximum nominal pore size of 0.2 µm.

### 4.6 Environmental and process monitoring

1. Include viable, non-viable, and APS results in the CCS. Use the results together; no single monitoring element demonstrates asepsis.
2. Establish a risk-based monitoring program that defines locations, frequency, methods, and incubation conditions. Consider the product and process, facility and equipment, critical steps, qualification and routine data, typical flora, and airflow visualization.
3. Monitor cleanrooms, clean-air equipment, and personnel during critical processing, including set-up. Monitoring locations, frequencies, methods, incubation conditions, and alert and action levels are those of the environmental monitoring program under SOP-QC-001, Environmental Monitoring; escalation and review follow that procedure's roles.
4. For Grade A, monitor the highest-risk locations throughout critical operations, including assembly.
5. Justify sampling locations and sampler positions. Sampling must not risk contamination or impair Grade A/B airflow.
6. Monitor surfaces at the end of operations.
7. Monitor personnel periodically, after critical interventions (at least gloves), and on each Grade B exit (gloves and gown).
8. Replace outer gloves after intervention sampling; if a gown is sampled, replace it before further activity.
9. Set and periodically review viable and total-particle alert levels.
10. Set action limits, which may be stricter than the maxima below.
11. Establish Grade D in-operation particle limits by risk assessment and routine data. At-rest particle limits apply after the qualification-defined clean-up period, with the area unmanned.

**Maximum total particles/m³ (≥0.5 µm / ≥5 µm):**

| Grade | At rest | In operation |
|---|---:|---:|
| A | 3,520 / 29 | 3,520 / 29 |
| B | 3,520 / 29 | 352,000 / 2,930 |
| C | 352,000 / 2,930 | 3,520,000 / 29,300 |
| D | 3,520,000 / 29,300 | Not predetermined |

12. For Grade A, monitor particles continuously at ≥0.5 µm and ≥5 µm, at a sampling rate of at least 28 litres per minute.
13. Correlate samples with limits frequently, alarm on alert-level exceedance, and define the response, including consideration of additional microbial monitoring.
14. Grade B monitoring must capture contamination increases and deterioration; alarm on alert-level exceedance.
15. Justify sample volumes.

**Viable action limits:**

| Grade | Air (CFU/m³) | Settle plate (CFU/4 hours) | Contact plate (CFU/plate) | Glove (CFU/glove) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| A | No growth | No growth | No growth | No growth |
| B | 10 | 5 | 5 | 5 |
| C | 100 | 50 | 25 | — |
| D | 200 | 100 | 50 | — |

16. Any growth in Grade A requires investigation.
17. Replace Grade A/B settle plates after a maximum of 4 hours.
18. Apply the Grade C/D settle-plate exposure time, up to a maximum of 4 hours, and monitoring frequency on a risk basis.
19. Trend excursions, consecutive alerts, recurring isolated action-limit excursions, and changes in microbial flora, especially spore-formers and moulds.
20. Assess and follow up alert exceedances, including consideration of investigation and CAPA.
21. Investigate action-limit exceedances.
22. Assess product impact of action-limit exceedances (including batches made between monitoring and reporting).
23. Implement CAPA for action-limit exceedances.
24. Identify Grade A/B isolates to species.
25. Assess batch impact of Grade A/B isolates.

### 4.7 Aseptic process simulations (media fills)

1. Use APS periodically to verify aseptic controls; do not use APS as the primary means of validation. A media fill represents the process at its worst credible case.
2. Include routine interventions, the maximum number of personnel, and the maximum process duration in the simulation; do not edit these conditions out of the simulation.
3. Use sterile nutrient medium or a surrogate that represents relevant product-risk characteristics and does not inhibit growth.
4. Before use, test APS media for growth promotion using a scientifically justified, designated group of reference microorganisms, including suitable representative local isolates. Testing should normally be performed by the end user. APS media are accepted for use only when growth-promotion testing passes with the designated organism panel under SOP-QC-001.
5. Justify outsourced or supplier testing and consider transport and shipping conditions.
6. Simulate all critical steps from sterilization or decontamination through sealing, including interventions and additional aseptic steps that cannot be performed through a filter.
7. Substitute air for inert gas unless the simulation is intended to represent anaerobic conditions.
8. Avoid separate unit-operation simulations unless justified and the simulations collectively cover the process.
9. Plan each APS to cover worst-case variables, container/closure combinations, maximum holding times, suitable fill volume and headspace, media and reference organisms, local isolates, detection method, duration, shifts, interruptions, monitoring throughout the simulation, and campaign start/end risks.
10. For sterile active substances, use a batch size representative of routine operations and include worst-case interventions and all product-contact surfaces.
11. Evaluate all simulation materials.
12. For initial validation, perform at least three consecutive satisfactory APS covering all shifts.
13. Repeat APS after significant changes assessed to affect sterility assurance.
14. Revalidate twice a year (approximately every six months) for each aseptic process, filling line, and shift.
15. Each operator must participate in at least one successful APS annually.
16. For manual operations, initially validate each container, closure, and equipment train; each operator must pass at least three consecutive APS, followed by one APS approximately every six months per operator.
17. Use an APS batch size that mimics routine operation. Fill typically at least 5,000 to 10,000 units. For production batches under 5,000 units, fill at least the production-batch size.
18. Justify the unit count in the CCS.
19. Agitate, swirl, or invert units before incubation.
20. Incubate all integral units.
21. Evaluate all integral units. Exclude discarded units only under the same clearly specified circumstances used for routine filling, and never exclude more units than routine production would discard.
22. Simulate and incubate discarded product-contact flushes unless it has been demonstrated that they do not affect sterility.
23. Incubate units promptly in clear containers under the conditions and for the duration validated in the APS protocol approved by Quality Assurance.
24. Trained, qualified personnel must inspect units.
25. Use suitable reference organisms and local isolates for positive controls.
26. Target zero growth. Any contaminated unit fails the APS.
27. Investigate the root cause of an APS failure.
28. Implement corrections for an APS failure.
29. Normally perform at least three successful consecutive repeat APS after an APS failure.
30. Review production records since the last successful APS.
31. Assess batch sterile-breach risk since the last successful APS.
32. Quarantine products made on the line after an APS failure until successful resolution.
33. Quality Assurance decides the disposition of quarantined product under SOP-QA-006, Batch Record Review and Product Release, and SOP-QA-010, with QA Manager approval for release of any affected batch; successful resolution means the root cause is identified, corrections are implemented, and the required repeat APS are successful.
34. Restrict an implicated operator until retrained and requalified.
35. Resume production only after successful revalidation, on QA Manager authorization.
36. Document the reconciliation of processed, incubated, and non-incubated units and the reasons for any exclusions.
37. Record all interventions with start and end times and personnel.
38. Record monitoring and testing data.
39. Abort an APS only when written procedures require commercial lots to be handled similarly, and document the investigation.
40. Repeat initial validation after extended inactivity or changes to the process, equipment, procedures, environment, or container/closure combinations that may affect asepsis.

### 4.8 Review, deviations, and changes

1. Review monitoring and APS results as part of process review and when investigating contamination-control concerns.
2. Investigate deviations, monitoring excursions, and APS failures for potential process and product impact under SOP-QA-010, Handling of Deviations; corrective and preventive action follows SOP-QA-011, Corrective and Preventive Action (CAPA).
3. Assess changes to systems that may affect contamination control before and after implementation under SOP-QA-004, Change Control.
4. Quality Assurance assesses the impact of each change on the CCS.
5. Update the CCS where appropriate.
6. Batch impact assessment and disposition follow SOP-QA-006.
