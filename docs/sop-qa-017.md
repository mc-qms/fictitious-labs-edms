# SOP-QA-017 — Cleaning Validation

**Fictitious Labs Inc.** · Version 1.0 · Effective 2026-03-02 · Owner: Quality Assurance

## 1. Purpose

Establish how cleaning processes for product-contact equipment are validated and verified, so that residues of the previous product, cleaning agents, and microorganisms are removed to scientifically justified limits.

## 2. Scope

Applies to the validation of cleaning for shared and dedicated product-contact equipment at Fictitious Labs Inc., a US-based biologics organization manufacturing monoclonal antibody drug substance in the United States, in the following functions: Manufacturing, Quality Assurance, Quality Control laboratory.

Does not cover routine cleaning operations themselves, which follow equipment-specific cleaning procedures, or cleaning of non-product-contact surfaces.

## 3. Responsibilities

- **Validation Engineer**
  - Plan and coordinate cleaning validation across the equipment and process lifecycle.
  - Coordinate risk assessments, worst-case selection, protocol preparation, and reporting of results against protocol criteria.
  - Document the rationale for study design and the conclusions supported by the results.
  - Coordinate assessment when a cleaning process is ineffective or unsuitable for equipment.

- **Manufacturing Operator**
  - Perform the cleaning steps required by the applicable equipment-specific cleaning procedure during validation and verification activities.
  - Provide information on cleaning-process variables, including manual operations and relevant timing, for the study risk assessment.

- **Laboratory Analyst**
  - Perform the analytical testing specified in the protocol. Use methods shown to be suitable for the intended measurements.
  - Document sampling and test results in the study records.

- **Quality Assurance**
  - Provide quality oversight throughout the cleaning validation lifecycle.
  - Authorize protocols before execution.
  - Approve reports.
  - Review deviations and proposed changes through the applicable quality processes.
  - Authorize decisions to proceed, including release of equipment to production after validation.

## 4. Procedure

### 4.1 Plan the validation

1. Plan cleaning validation across the lifecycle of the product, equipment, and cleaning process. Use a quality risk management approach under SOP-QA-009, Quality Risk Management, based on scientific knowledge.
2. Document the reasoning and results.
3. Reassess risks when new information or changes could affect the assessment.
4. Define the validation scope, equipment covered, cleaning process, study design, responsibilities, and acceptance criteria in a protocol before execution. Include the critical process attributes and parameters and specify how results will be evaluated.
5. The Validation Engineer and the Process Owner approve the protocol.
6. Quality Assurance authorizes the protocol; do not execute the protocol before it is approved and authorized.
7. Identify process factors that may affect cleaning effectiveness, including operator-dependent steps, the level of procedural detail, rinsing times, cleaning-process automation, utility and equipment operating ranges, and the time between manufacture and cleaning and between cleaning and use.
8. Use identified worst-case conditions as the basis for the study.
9. For automated cleaning, validate the specified normal operating ranges of the utilities and equipment.
10. Where similar equipment is grouped, document why the selected equipment represents the group. Consider equipment design and product-contact locations when defining the study scope.

### 4.2 Assess residue risks and set acceptance criteria

1. Assess carryover risks for residues of the previous product, cleaning agents, and microorganisms or endotoxins.
2. Set product-residue carryover limits using a toxicological evaluation of the previous product where the available data allow. Use a health-based exposure limit, generally a substance-specific Permitted Daily Exposure (PDE), rather than a customary fraction of a dose.
3. Document the limit rationale and supporting references in the risk assessment.
4. When deriving a PDE, review available human and animal data, identify critical effects, and justify the selected point of departure and adjustment factors. If more than one PDE is derived, justify the selected value; the lowest is usually used. Account for a different route of administration when route-specific bioavailability differs and the available data support a correction. If data are unavailable and the new route may increase systemic exposure, assume 100% contaminant bioavailability.
5. For therapeutic macromolecules and peptides, assess whether exposure to pH extremes or heat during cleaning is expected to degrade or denature the product. A PDE based on active, intact product may not be applicable in these circumstances.
6. Where a PDE based on active, intact product is not applicable, set the limit in the risk assessment from the validated analytical capability and a documented safety factor.
7. Assess other potential routes of cross-contamination case by case.
8. For a genotoxic active substance with no discernible threshold, a limit dose of 1.5 µg/person/day may be applied. Use a compound-specific acceptable intake when sufficient carcinogenicity data exist, and the PDE approach when a threshold-related mechanism is sufficiently supported.
9. Apply the product-residue limit as a maximum allowable carryover allocated across the equipment train in proportion to product-contact surface area, and consider the potential cumulative effect of residues across multiple items of equipment in the train.
10. Establish acceptance criteria for cleaning-agent residues from the agent manufacturer's toxicological data or a PDE, applied the same way.
11. Consider microbial and endotoxin contamination risks when developing the protocol. The company-set criteria for cleaned product-contact surfaces are: microbial recovery not more than 10 CFU/100 cm² by swab and not more than 10 CFU/100 mL by rinse, and rinse endotoxin not more than 0.25 EU/mL.
12. Select the worst-case product, equipment location, and operating condition for the study. The worst case must represent the hardest-to-clean product, location, and condition for the group. Consider factors such as solubility, cleanability, toxicity, and potency when selecting products.
13. Document the scientific rationale for each selection.
14. Assess the impact of new products on the selection.
15. For campaign manufacture, assess the effect of campaign length on cleanability.
16. Base the study on the maximum campaign length, expressed in time and/or number of batches.
17. Define dirty and clean hold times based on the effects of the time between manufacture and cleaning and the time between cleaning and use.

### 4.3 Define sampling and analytical methods

1. Specify the sampling locations, the rationale for selecting them, the sampling method, and the applicable acceptance criteria in the protocol before execution.
2. Select swabbing, rinsing, or another suitable method based on the equipment.
3. Show that the sampling materials and method do not affect results and that residue recovery is possible from each product-contact material and sampling method used.
4. Demonstrate sampling recovery for each material and method; a method with recovery below 50% is not used without a Quality-Assurance-approved justification and a correction factor, recorded in the protocol.
5. Use analytical methods suitable for the intended measurements.
6. Validate methods used for qualification, validation, or cleaning activities, including appropriate detection and quantification limits, which are stated in the protocol.
7. For microbial testing, confirm that the method supports recovery of microorganisms in the presence of relevant product or sanitizing agents, as applicable.

### 4.4 Execute and evaluate the study

1. Perform cleaning using the applicable equipment-specific cleaning procedure.
2. For manual cleaning, assess the effectiveness of the manual process.
3. For manual cleaning, document the effectiveness of the manual process.
4. For manual cleaning, define a justified frequency for confirming it.
5. Perform cleaning and sampling under the protocol-defined conditions.
6. Conduct the cleaning process an appropriate number of times, justified by risk assessment.
7. Evaluate each result against the predefined criteria. Include visual cleanliness as an acceptance criterion, but do not use visual inspection alone as evidence of acceptable cleaning.
8. Do not repeat cleaning and testing until acceptable residue results are obtained.
9. Record results that fail to meet predefined criteria as deviations.
10. Fully investigate each failure through SOP-QA-010, Handling of Deviations: the failure is initiated as a deviation record the same business day, investigated under that procedure.
11. The failure's implications for the validation are assessed and discussed in the report.
12. Document any significant change to an approved protocol during execution, including a change to acceptance criteria or operating parameters, as a deviation.
13. Scientifically justify any significant change to an approved protocol during execution.
14. Route planned changes that may affect validated status through SOP-QA-004, Change Control: the change is submitted as a change request.
15. The change is assessed using quality risk management.
16. The change is reviewed — including supporting data demonstrating the impact of the change.
17. The change is approved before implementation.
18. After implementation, evaluate the change's effectiveness where appropriate.
19. Assess the effect of a significant protocol change on the validation.
20. Document the assessment in the report. Do not change acceptance criteria without a documented scientific justification. Do not implement a protocol change before it is authorized.

### 4.5 Report, verify, and maintain the validated state

1. Summarize results against the protocol acceptance criteria in a report.
2. Document the review, conclusions, and recommendation regarding the validation outcome in a report.
3. Document any unresolved deviations.
4. Assess the impact of any unresolved deviations on the validation.
5. The Validation Engineer reviews the report.
6. Quality Assurance approves the report.
7. Quality Assurance authorizes any decision to proceed.
8. Where verification after each batch or campaign is used, gather chemical analysis evidence showing that residues of the previous product or cleaning agents meet the scientifically established limits.
9. Shared equipment is verified after each campaign, by rinse and/or swab sampling against the limits of section 4.2, before it is available for further use.
10. Quality Control reports the verification results.
11. Quality Assurance makes the availability decision.
12. Evaluate equipment and cleaning processes at least every 3 years, and on a trigger — a new product, a relevant change, or a failure trend — to confirm they remain in a state of control.
13. Where requalification is performed at a set interval, justify the interval.
14. Where requalification is performed at a set interval, define the evaluation criteria, and consider the effect of gradual changes over time.
15. Evaluate proposed changes that could affect cleaning effectiveness or validated status through SOP-QA-004, Change Control.
16. Use a risk assessment to determine whether validation, verification, or requalification is needed.
17. After implementation, assess whether the change achieved its intended effect where appropriate.
18. If a cleaning process is ineffective or unsuitable for particular equipment, the Validation Engineer coordinates an assessment of whether dedicated equipment or other appropriate controls are needed.
19. Quality Assurance decides the outcome of the assessment.
20. The decision and its basis are documented in FL-EDMS.
21. The decision and its basis are acted on through SOP-QA-004.

## 5. Records

Cleaning validation records, including risk assessments and limit derivations, protocols and their approvals, sampling and analytical records, deviation records, reports, campaign verification results and availability decisions, and periodic review records, retained under the retention requirements of SOP-QA-001, Good Documentation Practices.
