# SOP-QC-003 — Analytical Procedure Validation

**Fictitious Labs Inc.** · Version 1.0 · Effective 2026-03-02 · Owner: Quality Control

## 1. Purpose

Describe how analytical test methods are validated — the characteristics assessed, the studies performed, and the criteria applied to demonstrate each method suitable for its intended use.

## 2. Scope

Applies to the validation of analytical procedures used for release, stability, and in-process testing at Fictitious Labs Inc., a US-based biologics organization manufacturing monoclonal antibody drug substance in the United States, in the following functions: Quality Control laboratory, Quality Assurance.

Does not cover analytical procedure development, method transfer between laboratories, or verification of compendial procedures.

## 3. Responsibilities

- **Analyst**
  - Execute the validation studies assigned in the protocol, record data and results completely and contemporaneously, and draft the data summaries for the validation report.

- **Study Director (Protocol Owner)**
  - One role: the QC scientist who owns the validation. Define the procedure's intended use, select the performance characteristics and acceptance criteria, prepare the validation protocol, and prepare the validation report.

- **Quality Assurance**
  - Review and approve the validation protocol before validation studies begin and the validation report before the method is approved for use. Both approvals are recorded in FL-EDMS.

## 4. Procedure

### 4.1 Plan the validation

1. Define the analytical procedure's intended purpose, the attribute measured, and the type of result reported.
2. Before starting studies, prepare a validation protocol that identifies the performance characteristics to assess, the study design, and the acceptance criteria for each characteristic.
3. Quality Assurance approves the protocol, with the approval recorded in FL-EDMS, before validation studies begin.
4. Select characteristics and criteria based on the procedure's technology, intended purpose, reportable range, and applicable specifications or reporting limits.
5. Justify any characteristic omitted from the validation.
6. Use reference materials with documented identity and purity, or suitably characterised materials.
7. Development data may support validation when their relevance is justified. Established platform procedures may use an abbreviated study plan when scientifically justified.
8. Design replicate testing to reflect routine analysis.
9. Justify any difference from routine replicate testing.

### 4.2 Select performance characteristics

Assess characteristics that demonstrate the procedure is fit for its intended use. Use the following as a guide:

- For identity procedures, assess specificity.
- For quantitative impurity procedures, assess specificity, response and range, quantitation limit (QL), accuracy, and precision.
- For impurity limit tests, assess specificity and detection limit (DL).
- For assay, potency, and other quantitative measurements, assess specificity, response and range, accuracy, and precision.
- For other quantitative measurements, use the impurity-test approach when the range limit is close to the DL or QL; otherwise use the assay approach. Whether a range limit is close to the DL or QL is determined by the Study Director with Quality Assurance — as a guide, a limit within one order of magnitude of the DL or QL is close — and the selected approach and its justification are stated in the validation protocol.
- For procedures with technology-inherent characteristics, an alternative set of characteristics may be used if justified.
- If a procedure does not have sufficient specificity, use supporting procedures unless the approach is justified.
- Accuracy and precision may be evaluated together. Specificity, accuracy, and precision may be assessed in studies across the reportable range.
- Assess robustness during development where possible. If it was assessed during development, it need not be repeated during validation when the existing data support that decision.

The procedure-specific characteristics and their acceptance criteria are identified in the validation protocol for each procedure.

### 4.3 Define the reportable range and response

1. Derive the reportable range from the intended use and applicable specifications or reporting limits.
2. Include the applicable upper and lower specification or reporting limits.
3. Confirm the range using acceptable response, accuracy, and precision.
4. Evaluate response across the range using an appropriate calibration model. Where linearity is assessed, use appropriate statistical methods and provide a data plot, correlation coefficient or coefficient of determination, intercept, slope, and assessment of residual patterns. At least five appropriately distributed concentrations are recommended.
5. For a non-linear response, assess an appropriate model by non-linear regression and evaluate performance across the range against known or theoretical values.
6. Justify extrapolation when materials covering the full range cannot be generated.

Examples of ranges include:

- **Assay:** 80% of declared content, or 80% of the lower specification acceptance criterion, to 120% of declared content, or 120% of the upper specification acceptance criterion.
- **Potency:** 20% below the lowest specification acceptance criterion to 20% above the highest specification acceptance criterion.
- **Impurity:** the reporting threshold to 120% of the specification acceptance criterion.
- When assay and impurity are measured in one test using one standard, demonstrate linearity from the impurity reporting level to 120% of the assay specification acceptance criterion.

These are examples; select and justify the procedure-specific range in the protocol.

### 4.4 Assess specificity and selectivity

1. Demonstrate that other substances do not interfere with measurement of the analyte, or compare results with a well-characterised orthogonal procedure, preferably one using a different measurement principle.
2. For an identification procedure, show positive results for samples containing the analyte and negative results for samples without the analyte and for structurally similar or related materials.
3. For separation procedures, assess discrimination at appropriate levels, including resolution of the closest-eluting components.
4. For assay or potency procedures, assess interference from impurities and excipients. This may include spiking or stressing the product and showing that the assay result is unaffected.
5. For purity or impurity procedures, spike or stress the product and show that interference does not affect the result.
6. Confirm specificity for process-related impurities against the product.
7. For stability-indicating quantitative procedures, assess specificity using relevant degradation products. This may include spiked analytes and known interferences, physically or chemically stressed samples, and aged or stressed-stored product.
8. If impurities cannot be prepared or isolated, compare samples containing typical impurities or degradation products using an orthogonal procedure and justify the approach.
9. If a procedure lacks sufficient discrimination on its own, use supporting procedures unless the combined approach is justified.

### 4.5 Assess detection and quantitation limits

1. When applicable, estimate the DL or QL by visual evaluation, signal-to-noise, or standard deviation and slope.
2. For signal-to-noise estimates, a ratio of 3:1 is generally acceptable for the DL and at least 10:1 for the QL.
3. When using standard deviation and slope, calculate:
   - DL = 3.3σ/S
   - QL = 10σ/S
   where σ is the standard deviation of response and S is the calibration-curve slope.
4. Validate a calculated or extrapolated DL using suitable samples near or at the DL.
5. Confirm an estimated QL using suitable samples near or at the QL. The QL may instead be validated through accuracy and precision.
6. A QL confirmation study may be omitted when the QL is well below the reporting limit, for example approximately 10 times lower, if justified.
7. For impurity procedures, the QL must be equal to or below the reporting threshold.
8. The procedure-specific DL or QL acceptance criteria, where applicable, are specified in the validation protocol.

### 4.6 Assess accuracy and precision

1. Establish accuracy across the reportable range under routine conditions. Use comparison with a reference material, spiking, or comparison with an orthogonal procedure.
2. Use appropriate concentrations and determinations. An example is three concentrations with three replicates at each concentration.
3. Report mean percent recovery or difference from the true value with a 95% confidence interval (the default confidence level, unless the protocol justifies another), or use a justified alternative.
4. For quantitative assay and impurity procedures, assess precision using homogeneous authentic or prepared samples.
5. Assess repeatability using at least nine determinations across the range, such as three concentrations with three replicates each, or at least six determinations at 100% of the test concentration.
6. Assess intermediate precision using relevant random effects, such as days, environmental conditions, analysts, or equipment.
7. Justify the factors selected.
8. Reproducibility may be assessed through an inter-laboratory study. It is usually not required for submission but may be considered for standardisation or use at multiple sites.
9. Report standard deviation, relative standard deviation, and a 95% confidence interval (or the protocol's justified level), or a justified alternative.
10. If accuracy and precision are evaluated together, describe the approach.
11. Report the combined result using an appropriate prediction, tolerance, or confidence interval.
12. The procedure-specific accuracy and precision acceptance criteria are specified in the validation protocol.

### 4.7 Assess biological potency procedures

For a biological potency procedure, assess characteristics across the intended range using the studies appropriate to the method, selected by the Study Director and defined, with their acceptance criteria, in the validation protocol:

- For specificity, assess whether the dose-response meets response criteria and demonstrates similarity between analyte and reference, with no matrix interference for binding procedures. For cell-based procedures, show that cells alone do not produce a dose-response. Use forced degradation where needed.
- For repeatability, an example study uses at least three replicates at five or more levels across the range.
- For intermediate precision, assess different analysts, multiple independent preparations, days, and potency levels across the range, using normal laboratory variation.
- For accuracy, an example study assesses recovery against theoretical activity using at least three independent preparations at five or more levels across the range.
- Define the range as the lowest to highest relative potency that meets the accuracy, precision, and response criteria across at least five levels.
- Consider deliberate variations to plates, buffers, incubation, instruments, reaction times, reagent lots, and controls. For binding assays, also consider coating proteins and capture or detection antibodies. For cell assays, also consider cell density, effector-to-target ratio, and cell generation number.

### 4.8 Assess robustness and system suitability

1. Assess robustness through deliberate variations to procedure parameters that could affect performance during the intended period of use. Consider sample-preparation and reagent stability for the duration of the procedure. The procedure-specific robustness assessment is defined in the validation protocol.
2. Document the robustness outcome. Data from development studies may support the assessment when relevant.
3. Include system suitability testing (SST) as a regular performance check in the analytical procedure.
4. Define SST materials, attributes, and acceptance criteria in the validation protocol, based on procedure performance, and carry them into the approved procedure.
5. When applicable, define sample suitability criteria. A satisfactory SST and acceptable sample suitability are prerequisites for a valid result.

### 4.9 Evaluate multivariate procedures, when applicable

1. Define performance factors, model assumptions, and the intended range of model applicability.
2. Select samples to represent relevant sources of variability.
3. Distribute them appropriately across the range.
4. Use reference values from reference procedures or prepared samples with known values.
5. Justify the reference approach and address uncertainty in reference values and homogeneity of prepared samples.
6. Use calibration data and internal testing to develop and assess the model.
7. Validate the model with independent samples not used in calibration or internal testing.
8. Document and justify data transformations, variable selection, and the number of latent variables.
9. Confirm the selected number during internal testing.
10. Assess prediction residuals across the working range.
11. For identification libraries, challenge the procedure with samples absent from the library.
12. Define a model maintenance plan in the validation protocol, including the model-specific outlier diagnostic procedures and limits, the frequency and circumstances for confirmatory testing, and the maintenance criteria.
13. In routine use, monitor the appropriateness of each measurement using outlier diagnostics.
14. Periodically and when specified events occur, compare predictions with known reference-sample values or reference-procedure results.
15. Reassess the model if confirmatory testing or outlier diagnostics fail predefined criteria, or if trending indicates potential unacceptable model, process, or material performance.
16. QC laboratory management, with Quality Assurance, decides whether the model and affected results may continue to be used; affected results are held from reporting until that decision is recorded in FL-EDMS.

### 4.10 Review and report validation results

1. Summarise study results in a validation report.
2. Compare each result with its protocol acceptance criteria.
3. Document the rationale for use of prior knowledge or development data and for any omitted or modified study.
4. A study result that does not meet its acceptance criteria is handled as a deviation under SOP-QA-010, Handling of Deviations, before the report is approved: Quality Assurance decides the disposition — repeat the study, justify the result, or narrow the validated claim — and the method is not approved for the failed characteristic until the deviation is dispositioned.
5. For a change to a validated procedure, determine the need for partial or full revalidation using a science- and risk-based justification under SOP-QA-009, Quality Risk Management. The Study Director decides the extent of revalidation with Quality Assurance; the decision and its justification are documented in the change record under SOP-QA-004, Change Control.

## 5. Records

Analytical procedure validation records, including approved protocols, study data and summaries, deviation records, and approved validation reports, retained under the retention requirements of SOP-QA-001, Good Documentation Practices.
